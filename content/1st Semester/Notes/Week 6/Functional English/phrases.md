# Phrases

Phrases are a group of words which perform a specific function in a sentence i.e. as a part of speech. It is a grammatical unit centered around a head word. It functions within a clause but can never be a clause itself. 

***A phrase can never make a sentence on its own in a formal prose.***

Phrases can't have both a noun and a verb. Yes, phrases **can contain** verbs. But they can only contain [[finite and infinitive#Finite Verbs|finite verbs]] (which include, but not limited to, [[1st Semester/Week 6/gerunds|Gerunds]] and [[1st Semester/Week 6/participles|Participles]]).
Let's look at a few examples: <br>
- #### Examples
	- She was standing \[in the big room\]
	Here \[in the big room\] is a **prepositional phrase working as a adverb** for \[standing\].
	Head word: in<br><br>
	- \[The students of class 9\] are very arrogant.
	Here \[The students of class 9] is a **noun phrases acting as the subject**.
	Head word: Students<br><br>
	- She \[has been reading\] a lot these days.
	Here \[has been reading] is a **verb phrase**.
	Head word: Reading<br><br>
	- I love \[every creation of God].
	Here, \[every creation of God] is another **noun phrase but this time acting as an object** to \[love].
	Head word: creation<br><br>
	- He entered the room, \[radiating pure positivity].
	Here, \[radiating pure positivity] is a **verb-based phrase acting as a compliment/adjective** to \[He].
	This phrase utilizes a [[participles|participle]] \[radiating]
	Head word: radiating<br><br>
	- That seminar was \[surprisingly astonishing].
	Here, \[surprisingly astonishing]  is a an **adjectivial phrase, acting as a compliment** to \[The seminar].
	Ik, it starts with an adverb. But it is functioning as an adjective and its head word is also:
	Head word: astonishing

<br>

Pretty simple, right?
A phrase has one head word, no finite verbs, no noun-verb pair and acts as a part of speech.
<br>
In ***rare cases*** though, when phrases contain [[clauses|clauses]] within them, they ***can contain both a noun and a verb*** (a [[finite and infinitive#FInite Verbs|finite]] one at that!).
Yup, that's possible too. Let's look at an example:
- #### Example
	- \[The bridge that connects the two villages] is old.<br>
	Here, the phrase \[The bridge that connects the two villages] contains the [[clauses#Relative Clause|relative clause]] \[that connects the two villages] which contains the [[finite and infinitive#Finite Verbs|finite verb]] \[connects].<br>
	But at the end of the day, the phrase structure is simple:
	"The bridge \[modifier]"
	and it acts as a part of speech (subject).<br>
	So this is a noun phrase acting as a subject.
