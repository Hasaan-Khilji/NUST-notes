# Infinite Limits

Remember when we studied this limit?$$\lim_{x \to 0}f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0}\frac{1}{x}$$
We said that the limit is ==🔴undefined==, because when we approached 0 from the left, f(x) approached $- \infty$ and when we approached from the right, f(x) approached $+\infty$.
OK, fair enough, limits *only exist* when right and left hand limits are the same.

But what if the right and left hand limits both approached infinity in the same direction? Like in $$\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{|x|}$$
Surely, the left and right hand limits are the same. But f(x) isn't really approaching a ***finite*** number, it's approaching ***infinity***. Can we say that as x approaches 0, f(x) approaches **infinity**?
The answer is ==🟢**YES**==. We can write: $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{|x|} =  +\infty$$
But by writing this, we are ***not*** saying that the limit exists. Nor are we saying that there is a real number $+\infty$; such a number does not exist.
By writing the above equation, we are just describing the behavior of a function whose values becomes arbitrarily large and positive as x approaches 0.

Moving away from [[asymptotes|horizontal and oblique asymptotes]], which were basically the behavior of functions as their independent variables approached infinity.
Infinite limits are the when the actual values of the functions approach infinities (+ve or -ve) as their input variable approaches some number. So basically:
$$\lim_{x \to c} f(x) = + \infty \\\\ or \\\\ \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = - \infty$$
Infinite limits can also be one sided: 
$$\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1}{x} = + \infty$$

Wherever there is an infinite limit, there is a [[1st Semester/Notes/Calculus-I/Week 6/vertical asymptotes|vertical asymptote]].
